Ibidapo Lawal and the Courage to Learn Again

He had the ambition. Experience supplied the hard lessons. Business school became a turning point. The Neveah founder’s story asks what it takes to build differently the next time.

There is a version of the entrepreneurial story that begins at the breakthrough. The company has a name, the founder has a biography, and the difficult years have been compressed into a sentence about perseverance. Ibidapo Lawal’s story becomes more interesting when you resist that compression.

Before Neveah, there were earlier businesses. Before the polished account of growth, there were setbacks. And somewhere along the way came a decision to return to the classroom—a reminder that ambition can take a man a long way without answering every question he needs to ask.

The ambition came first

Opeyemi Awoyemi, who identifies Lawal as his cousin in a public reflection on his journey, describes a man whose appetite for building was apparent early. Wealth arrived young, he writes, but it did not arrive with a guarantee of permanence. His account describes Lawal as having been bankrupt at least twice.

That detail changes the rhythm of the success story. It introduces a more demanding subject than confidence: the ability to begin again after the evidence has become uncomfortable. To build is to make decisions before the outcome is clear. To rebuild is to do it with a memory of what the last attempt cost.

Awoyemi places a significant turn in that journey at business school in the United States. In his telling, formal education joined an existing mixture of drive, practical intelligence and experience. Watching that combination develop helped shape his own determination to pursue further education.

A different kind of investment

Lawal’s educational path took him from Ibadan to Lead City University, where he studied Economics, and then to Drexel University for an MBA. Both Neveah’s management biography and The Guardian’s October 2024 profile record that progression.

For an entrepreneur, going back to school is an investment with an unusual admission built into it: there are things I do not yet know. Experience brings knowledge, but it can also harden a habit into a rule. A classroom creates an opportunity to place those rules beside other ways of thinking.

The value of that opportunity is not that a degree removes risk. It is that learning can improve the questions brought to a decision. What is the business actually earning? Which risks are being rewarded, and which are simply being repeated? What survives when the founder is no longer in every room? These are questions for anyone trying to turn personal drive into an enduring institution.

Building beyond the founder

The Guardian traces Lawal’s business activity through IAL Resources and Activo Global Investments, established in 2007, before Neveah’s arrival in 2014. Its profile describes a company exporting solid minerals and agricultural products. Neveah’s own account now places industrial recycling alongside its commodities business.

Those businesses make the story tangible. This is an ambition expressed through products, buyers, exports and industrial activity. The work is less romantic than the language often used to describe entrepreneurship. It involves turning a promise into something another person can depend on, then doing it again.

That is where a founder’s development becomes more than a private achievement. A company is a set of commitments to people who did not share the founder’s original leap of faith: employees, customers, partners and families. Growth increases the number of people affected by the quality of the decisions at the top.

What a setback is for

It is easy to admire resilience after the turnaround. It is harder to recognise the quieter work that makes a turnaround useful: accepting criticism, revising an assumption, asking someone better informed, abandoning a method that once felt inseparable from your identity.

Lawal’s journey offers a way into that conversation. The appeal is not a promise that every failure contains a future fortune. Some mistakes are costly, and some opportunities do not return. The more useful possibility is that a man can take responsibility for what experience has taught him and allow it to change the next attempt.

The next version of ambition

For men taught to equate competence with always knowing the answer, becoming a student again can feel like a loss of standing. Yet a willingness to learn may be one of the strongest expressions of seriousness about the work. The objective is bigger than appearing naturally gifted at it.

The lasting question in Lawal’s story is therefore not simply how much he wanted to succeed. It is what he was willing to change in pursuit of building something that could last. Ambition supplied the movement. Learning gave the journey another direction.
